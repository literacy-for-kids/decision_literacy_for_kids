# Worked Examples and Optional Depth

Use these cards after the corresponding core teaching. They supply the scenario and a sample response; no external research, new account, real-world contact, or private disclosure is required. The examples are fictional teaching cases, not real measurements, medical guidance, legal rulings, financial advice, or promises of outcomes.

## Two kinds of optional work

- **Supplied practice:** the scenario, sample response, and depth question are on this page. Allow about 15–20 minutes for one card, with paper or oral explanation. Read the card before the session; a calculator is optional for arithmetic.
- **Open research prompt:** a request to locate or construct missing teaching material, such as outside sources, interview records, or a real-case packet. This is not supplied instruction. A tool activity with complete steps is still supplied instruction, even when an adult must prepare the device or account. An adult must select and verify materials, arrange access, and plan additional time first. Use the supplied card instead when those materials are unavailable.

Choose depth by readiness and interest, not age alone. Learners aged 10–12 can use the core teaching without taking every technical add-on; older-learner work remains optional. The depth questions are not prerequisites for the next core week or criteria for its checkpoint.

## How to use a card

1. Read the scenario and ask for a reasoned answer.
2. Let the learner draw, speak, sort, or write using the fictional case.
3. Compare with the sample: notice the evidence and the limits, not matching wording.
4. If ready, change the stated assumption using the depth question.
5. If the core idea is unclear, return to the linked lesson before adding complexity. A private or paper artifact is enough.

Sample responses illustrate reasoning, not scripts to memorize or the only defensible answer to an open choice. They do not require a learner to experience or disclose the situation.

## Week 1
**Core lesson:** [Week 1: The Coin Flip Lab](./week01-week-1.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A learner chooses heads on a stated fair coin and loses one flip. Was it necessarily a poor choice?

**Illustrative response:** No. Either side had probability 1/2 under the model. A single outcome reflects chance and does not establish decision quality.

**Optional depth:** Separate a factor the learner controls from one they do not. No money or possessions are wagered.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 2
**Core lesson:** [Week 2: Process vs. Outcome](./week02-week-2.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional forecast gives 70% chance of rain. A learner takes an umbrella, but it stays dry. Evaluate the process.

**Illustrative response:** The learner used relevant information before the outcome. Dry weather was still possible, so one dry day does not prove the process was bad.

**Optional depth:** Name information known before the choice versus learned afterward; avoid grading a choice only by hindsight.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 3
**Core lesson:** [Week 3: Thinking in Probabilities](./week03-week-3.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** In 30 rolls of a modeled fair six-sided die, sixes appear 3 times. Does the model require exactly 5?

**Illustrative response:** No. The expected count is 30 × 1/6 = 5, but actual small samples vary. Three alone does not prove fairness or unfairness.

**Optional depth:** Explain why a 50% event estimate differs from "I have no information"; uncertainty does not automatically make an event a coin flip.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 4
**Core lesson:** [Week 4: The Decision Journal](./week04-week-4.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Write a journal entry before a fictional picnic choice, using a 40% rain estimate. What belongs before and after?

**Illustrative response:** Before: options, evidence, estimate, and reason for the choice. After: outcome, what changed, and a process reflection without rewriting the original belief.

**Optional depth:** Add one fact that would change the estimate; keep the example fictional and the original record intact.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 5
**Core lesson:** [Week 5: Meet Your Brain's Shortcuts](./week05-week-5.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A sign first shows $100, then $40. Another sign just shows $40 for the same item. What might the first number do?

**Illustrative response:** The $100 can act as an anchor, making $40 feel small. Check the item’s useful value and comparable options, not only the displayed discount.

**Optional depth:** Explain when a shortcut saves time and when checking evidence is worth the effort.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 6
**Core lesson:** [Week 6: The Loss Aversion Lab](./week06-week-6.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Choose between keeping 4 pretend points for sure or a fair-coin outcome of 8 or 0. Are the averages different?

**Illustrative response:** Both modeled averages are 4. Someone may reasonably prefer certainty; declining the risky option does not by itself prove a bias.

**Optional depth:** Describe how framing the same outcomes as gains or losses might change feelings without changing the arithmetic.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 7
**Core lesson:** [Week 7: The Sunk Cost Trap](./week07-week-7.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional craft project took two hours and no longer meets its goal. Is the time already spent a reason to spend two more?

**Illustrative response:** Past time cannot be recovered. Compare future benefit and future cost; continuing may make sense only for reasons about what happens next.

**Optional depth:** Add a useful future benefit, such as learning a needed skill, and see whether the preferred choice changes.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 8
**Core lesson:** [Week 8: Bias Hunters](./week08-week-8.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional ad says "Only smart people choose this" and shows a crossed-out price. Identify two things to check.

**Illustrative response:** The identity appeal is not evidence of quality; the old price may anchor judgment. Check useful features, full cost, and alternatives.

**Optional depth:** Rewrite it as a claim that could be tested rather than a label about the buyer.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 9
**Core lesson:** [Week 9: Expected Value](./week09-week-9.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Outdoor enjoyment is 10 if dry and 0 if rainy; indoor is 6 either way. Compare rain estimates of 20% and 60%.

**Illustrative response:** Outdoor EV is 8 at 20% rain and 4 at 60%; indoor stays 6. The recommendation changes, so the estimate matters and the model omits other constraints.

**Optional depth:** Find the 40% crossover and name one safety or access factor that an enjoyment score should not override.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 10
**Core lesson:** [Week 10: Signal vs. Noise](./week10-week-10.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Three invented same-length trips take 10, 14, and 12 minutes. A fourth takes 30 because the road closes. What can you infer?

**Illustrative response:** The first three average 12. The closure matters to predicting future trips if it remains; an outlier is not automatically meaningless noise.

**Optional depth:** Describe one reason to keep the fourth result and one reason to analyze ordinary trips separately, clearly stating the purpose.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 11
**Core lesson:** [Week 11: Reversible vs. Irreversible](./week11-week-11.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional learner can try a draft title privately or permanently publish private information. Which is easier to reverse?

**Illustrative response:** A private draft title can be changed at low cost. Published private information may be copied, so deleting the original may not reverse the harm.

**Optional depth:** Choose a small reversible rehearsal before a larger commitment, with adult help when needed.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 12
**Core lesson:** [Week 12: Your Choices Affect Others](./week12-week-12.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Two fictional groups share one room; one group’s loud game prevents the other from reading. Who bears a cost?

**Illustrative response:** The readers bear an effect of a choice they did not make. A plan should consider both groups, not only the game players’ benefit.

**Optional depth:** Suggest a time or space adjustment and describe what would count as a workable trial for each group.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 13
**Core lesson:** [Week 13: The Prisoner's Dilemma](./week13-week-13.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** In a classroom point game, both cooperate gives 3 each; one defects gets 5 while the other gets 0; both defect gives 1 each. Compare outcomes.

**Illustrative response:** Mutual cooperation has total 6, while one-sided defection totals 5 and mutual defection totals 2. Individual incentives can conflict with a shared outcome.

**Optional depth:** Explain why this invented payoff table does not justify cooperating with threats or abuse; safety and power are outside this game model.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 14
**Core lesson:** [Week 14: The Commons Simulation](./week14-week-14.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A pretend pond starts with 12 tokens. Each round removes 6 and replaces 4. Track two rounds.

**Illustrative response:** After round 1 it has 10; after round 2 it has 8. Removal exceeds replacement, so the modeled stock declines.

**Optional depth:** Test a rule removing only 4 per round. State that equal replenishment is an assumption, not a real fish-management rule.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 15
**Core lesson:** [Week 15: Identify Your Friction Point](./week15-week-15.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional club forgets supplies at three meetings. Name a recurring friction point without blaming a person.

**Illustrative response:** The preparation system fails to make supplies visible before the meeting. A reminder or checklist is a possible intervention to test.

**Optional depth:** Specify an observable goal, such as all listed supplies present at start, rather than "be more responsible."

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 16
**Core lesson:** [Week 16: Design Your Decision Protocol](./week16-week-16.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Design a fictional reminder rule: the checklist is reviewed before packing. What makes it a protocol?

**Illustrative response:** It states when to act, what to check, and how to tell whether packing is complete. It turns a general goal into repeatable steps.

**Optional depth:** Add a fallback when a supply is unavailable; do not assume reminders solve every access problem.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 17
**Core lesson:** [Week 17: Test and Collect Data](./week17-week-17.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** In a fictional three-meeting trial, supplies were complete at two starts, compared with one of three before. Evaluate it.

**Illustrative response:** It improved from 1/3 to 2/3 in these small samples. That is a useful clue, not proof that the protocol caused the change or will always work.

**Optional depth:** Name a confounder, such as easier supply lists, and one comparable next trial.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 18
**Core lesson:** [Week 18: Patch, Present, Reflect](./week18-week-18.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** The checklist trial missed one item because its list was outdated. What is a useful patch and reflection?

**Illustrative response:** Update the list and assign a check before each meeting. Explain what failed and test the new version instead of treating one miss as total failure.

**Optional depth:** Present the change with its evidence and limits; a fictional artifact or private explanation is enough.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.
