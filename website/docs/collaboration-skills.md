# Collaboration Skills for Group Decisions

Decision Literacy often includes group choices, shared tradeoffs, probability games, decision journals, and reflection. Collaboration helps kids define the shared decision, choose roles, compare options fairly, and review the outcome without blaming one person.

This page is the local doorway into the Literacy for Kids [Collaboration Toolkit](https://www.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/toolkits/collaboration), connected to the clear thinking this curriculum builds.

## A few core ideas

- **A group decision needs a shared goal.** "What are we deciding together?" comes before comparing options.
- **Roles help people compare options without chaos.** Option finder, risk spotter, recorder, and question asker are all real jobs.
- **Fair discussion gives quieter voices a way in.** The loudest voice is not automatically the best option.
- **Reviewing the outcome together helps the group learn.** A group can reflect on its process without blaming one person.

## When this shows up

- When a group is choosing between options
- When people value different tradeoffs
- When one person dominates the choice
- When the group needs to compare pros, cons, risks, and probabilities
- When reviewing whether a decision process worked

## Tools that help

- **Shared decision goal** — "What are we deciding together?"
- **Role check** — option finder, risk spotter, recorder, question asker.
- **Fair voice check** — has everyone had a way to weigh in?
- **Disagree and build frame** — "My concern is ___ because our goal is ___."
- **Finish together review** — what worked, who helped, what should change?

:::tip Collaboration Moment
A group decision works better when everyone knows the shared goal. Before comparing options, ask: "What are we trying to decide together?" That keeps the group from arguing about different problems.
:::

:::caution Everyday collaboration skills, not unsafe conflict management
These are everyday collaboration tools, not therapy, bullying intervention, conflict mediation, legal advice, or a replacement for trusted adults, educators, caregivers, or qualified support. Kids should not be expected to manage unsafe, coercive, bullying, discriminatory, or threatening group situations alone. If group work feels unsafe, targeted, or too big, involve a trusted adult.
:::
