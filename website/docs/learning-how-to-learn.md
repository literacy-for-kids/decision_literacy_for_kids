# Learning How to Learn for Better Decisions

This curriculum teaches kids to make decisions under uncertainty. Learning how to learn helps them revisit outcomes, separate luck from skill, learn from decision journals, and improve their process over time.

This page is the local doorway into the Literacy for Kids [Learning How to Learn Toolkit](https://www.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/toolkits/learning-how-to-learn), connected to the clear thinking this curriculum builds.

## A few core ideas

- **A decision result is feedback, not a final grade on your intelligence.** One outcome does not measure how smart you are.
- **Practice helps you recognize bias and uncertainty faster.** Spotting patterns is a skill that improves with repetition.
- **Decision journals help you learn from choices over time.** Patterns show up across many decisions, not just one.
- **Mistakes and unlucky outcomes are different.** A good choice can have a bad result, and that is worth noticing.

## When this shows up

- When a decision turns out differently than expected
- When you are learning probability or expected value
- When a bias is hard to notice
- When a decision journal feels repetitive
- When feedback could improve your next choice

## Tools that help

- **"What did this outcome teach me?"** — treat the result as information, not a verdict.
- **Mistake vs. luck reflection** — was the process off, or was the outcome just unlucky?
- **Retrieval practice for key terms** — practice remembering probability and bias vocabulary.
- **Decision journal review** — revisit past entries to find patterns.
- **Practice plan** — pick one decision skill and practice it in small steps.

:::tip Learning Moment
After a decision result, ask: "What did this teach me about my process?" Learning from decisions means looking for patterns over time, not judging yourself from one outcome.
:::

:::caution Everyday learning skills, not a diagnosis
These are everyday learning tools, not therapy, medical advice, disability evaluation, or a replacement for trusted adults, educators, caregivers, or qualified support. Kids should not be treated as lazy or broken when learning is hard. If learning feels consistently overwhelming, inaccessible, unsafe, or severely distressing, involve a trusted adult and appropriate support.
:::
